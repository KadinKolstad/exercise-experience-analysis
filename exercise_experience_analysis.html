Data Source

Dataset: “Gym Members Exercise Dataset” by Vala Khorasani. Source: https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/valakhorasani/gym-members-exercise-dataset

Research Question

Is there a difference in mean calories burned per hour between novice and experienced male exercisers in this dataset?

I chose this question because I’m interested in exercise and wanted to see how calorie expenditure compares across experience levels. Since workout lengths vary, I calculated calories burned per hour rather than comparing total calories burned per session. I used experience level 1 as the novice group and level 3 as the experienced group, excluding level 2 to focus on the lowest and highest experience categories.

This comparison looks at reported calorie expenditure relative to workout duration. It does not directly measure fitness, workout quality, or progress over time.

Study Design and Hypotheses

I compared calories burned per hour for 197 male records with experience level 1 and 101 male records with experience level 3. The novice group averaged 755.43 calories per hour, while the experienced group averaged 759.30. Throughout the analysis, I calculated the difference as experienced minus novice, giving an observed difference of 3.87 calories per hour.

This is an analysis of an existing dataset. I did not assign experience levels or conduct an experiment. Because I have not verified the original collection and sampling methods, I cannot assume the records represent all male gym-goers. The statistical results should be interpreted with that limitation in mind.

The null hypothesis is that the two groups have equal mean calories burned per hour. The alternative hypothesis is that their means differ.

\[H_0: \mu_{\text{Experience 1 CalPerHour}} = \mu_{\text{Experience 3 CalPerHour}}\]

\[H_1: \mu_{\text{Experience 1 CalPerHour}} \ne \mu_{\text{Experience 3 CalPerHour}}\]

Permutation Test

obsDiff <- mean(experiencedMales) - mean(noobieMales)

malesCalPerHour <- c(experiencedMales, noobieMales)
N <- 10000
diffMeans <- numeric(N)

for (i in 1:N){
  index <- sample(length(malesCalPerHour), size = length(experiencedMales), replace = F)
  diffMeans[i] <- mean(malesCalPerHour[index]) - mean(malesCalPerHour[-index])
}
sum(abs(diffMeans) >= abs(obsDiff)) / N
## [1] 0.7058

I performed 10,000 random shuffles while keeping the original group sizes. The permutation p-value was approximately 0.70, meaning about 70% of the shuffles produced an absolute difference in means at least as large as the observed difference of 3.87 calories per hour.

At a significance level of 0.05, I fail to reject the null hypothesis. This test does not provide strong evidence of a difference in mean calories burned per hour between the two groups. It does not establish that their means are equal.

Bootstrap Analysis

N <- 10000
bootDiff <- numeric(N)

for (i in 1:N) {
  bootExp <- sample(experiencedMales, size = length(experiencedMales), replace = TRUE)
  bootNoob <- sample(noobieMales, size = length(noobieMales), replace = TRUE)
  
  bootDiff[i] <- mean(bootExp) - mean(bootNoob)
}

hist(bootDiff, main = "Bootstrap Distribution of Mean Differences", xlab = "Experienced minus novice (calories per hour)"
)

qqnorm(bootDiff, main = "Normal Q-Q Plot of Bootstrap Differences")

qqline(bootDiff, lwd = 3, col = "red")

The bootstrap histogram is roughly symmetric, and the points in the normal Q-Q plot follow a reasonably straight line. This suggests that the bootstrap distribution of mean differences is approximately normal. These plots describe the shape of the resampling distribution; they do not establish whether the difference between groups is statistically significant.

Bootstrap Bias and Standard Error

(bias <- mean(bootDiff) - obsDiff)
## [1] -0.08839391
(se <- sd(bootDiff))
## [1] 9.925263

The estimated bootstrap bias is small compared with the bootstrap standard error, meaning the average bootstrap difference is close to the observed difference. The standard error estimates how much the difference in sample means varies across the bootstrap samples.

These results help describe uncertainty in the estimate. They do not verify that the original records are independent or representative of a broader population.

Bootstrap Percentile Confidence Interval

(bootCI <- quantile(bootDiff, c(0.025, 0.975)))
##      2.5%     97.5% 
## -15.62681  23.16514

I used the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap differences to construct a 95% bootstrap percentile confidence interval. The difference is measured as experienced minus novice, in calories burned per hour.

The interval includes zero and extends into both negative and positive values. Under the assumptions of the method, it is compatible with either group having the higher mean. The interval therefore does not provide clear evidence of a difference between the groups.

Welch Two-Sample Test

t.test(experiencedMales, noobieMales, alternative = "two.sided", conf.level = 0.95)
## 
##  Welch Two Sample t-test

## data:  experiencedMales and noobieMales
## t = 0.39054, df = 214.43, p-value = 0.6965
## alternative hypothesis: true difference in means is not equal to 0
## 95 percent confidence interval:
##  -15.66331  23.40373
## sample estimates:
## mean of x mean of y 
##  759.2973  755.4271

The Welch two-sample t-test produced a p-value of approximately 0.697 and a 95% confidence interval from -15.66 to 23.40 calories per hour for the experienced-minus-novice mean difference.

Because the p-value is greater than 0.05 and the interval includes zero, I fail to reject the null hypothesis. Although the experienced group’s sample mean was 3.87 calories per hour higher, the analysis does not provide strong evidence of a difference. The confidence interval remains compatible with differences in either direction.

Conclusions

The bootstrap percentile interval and the Welch confidence interval give similar results, and both include zero. Both methods therefore lead to the same overall interpretation: this analysis does not provide strong evidence of a difference in mean calories burned per hour between the novice and experienced groups. Agreement between the methods does not establish that the groups are equivalent or remove limitations in the underlying data.

Limitations

This analysis focuses on male records in experience levels 1 and 3, so its findings do not describe female records or the intermediate experience group. It also does not adjust for workout type, body weight, age, or other factors that could affect calorie expenditure.

The tests treat the records as independent, and the permutation test assumes group labels are exchangeable under its null hypothesis. I have not verified the dataset’s original collection process or sampling method, which limits how confidently these results can be applied beyond the dataset. I would treat this project as an application of statistical comparison and resampling methods rather than a general conclusion about exercise experience.
